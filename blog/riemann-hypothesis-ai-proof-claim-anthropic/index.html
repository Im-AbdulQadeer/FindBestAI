Did AI Just Solve Math’s Most Famous 167-Year-Old Mystery? Inside the Anthropic Mathematician’s Riemann Hypothesis Claim

On November 20, 2026, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, posted a four-page note saying that, working with Claude, he had discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, the best-known open problem in mathematics. The note comes with no human-readable paper, only a huge Lean code archive. Andrew Sutherland of MIT reportedly compiled the code, and others are still checking whether it really proves the hypothesis as stated. This is a claim, not an accepted proof, and Anthropic had not issued an official statement in the coverage we read. This guide explains what was claimed, what has and hasn't been checked, what experts are saying and what could happen next. Everything here is as of November 21, 2026.

⚡ DIRECT ANSWER: Did AI Just Solve the Riemann Hypothesis?

Not yet confirmed. Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge claims he and Claude proved the Riemann Hypothesis, released as a roughly 175,000-file Lean archive on November 20, 2026. Early reports say the Lean code compiles on independent machines, but a final check that the code proves the hypothesis as stated was still pending, there is no human-readable proof, and nobody has refereed it. The problem remains listed as unsolved by the Clay Mathematics Institute, whose rules require publication and a waiting period before any prize.

What Is the Riemann Hypothesis?

The Riemann Hypothesis is a statement about a function called the Riemann zeta function, introduced by Bernhard Riemann in an 1859 paper. The function has "zeros," points where its value is zero. The hypothesis says that all the interesting ("nontrivial") zeros lie on one vertical line, where the real part of the input equals one-half. That line is the dashed one on our thumbnail.

It matters because the zeros are tied to how prime numbers (2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and so on) are spread out. If the hypothesis is true, mathematicians get much tighter control over that pattern, and many other results that assume it would become theorems. It has been open for about 167 years. The Clay Mathematics Institute lists it as one of its seven Millennium Prize Problems, each carrying a $1 million prize.

What Has and Hasn't Been Checked

"It compiles" and "it's proven" are different things. Lean is a proof assistant: it checks that each logical step follows from the last. That is powerful, but it can only check the statement it was given.

The main worry the sources raise is subtle. If the Lean definitions of the zeta function or the hypothesis don't match the classical ones, the code could be flawlessly "proving" something slightly different. That is what the pending comparator check and the redefinition checks are meant to rule out. One aggregator we read also listed several extra kernel checks, but we couldn't confirm those elsewhere, so we haven't included them.

Why It Isn't "Solved" Yet

  • The statement hasn't been fully confirmed. The comparator check was pending in the reports we read.
  • Humans haven't read it. Mathematics normally runs on explanation and refereeing. Here, understanding is still to come.
  • The Clay rules take time. Per Kingy.ai, Clay's page still listed the problem as unsolved on November 20, and its rules call for a qualifying publication, a two-year wait and general community acceptance before any prize is considered. We didn't read Clay's rules directly.
  • It's a claim from one researcher. Not a company announcement, and not yet a community consensus.

That said, it's not a typical crank claim. The author is a working mathematician, the code has reportedly been compiled by respected researchers, and several experts sound cautiously positive. "Unverified but serious" is the fair description.

Don't Confuse It With the August Result

In August, Anthropic published a different result. An unreleased Claude model tried the Riemann Hypothesis, gave up on it, and instead raised the proven lower bound on the share of zeros on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2%. Anthropic wrote that it didn't expect the techniques to lead to a proof of the hypothesis, and James Maynard called the blog post "remarkably restrained" (Scientific American). The November claim is a different, much bigger and far less checked assertion. If you saw "Claude and the Riemann Hypothesis" headlines in August, those were about the partial result.

What It Means for AI and Mathematics

If the claim holds, it would be the most striking example yet of AI contributing to research mathematics. Even if it fails, the episode shows a real shift: proofs that are checkable by machine but not yet understood by people. The Next Web notes that the Leiden Declaration earlier this year, signed by prominent mathematicians, argued proofs should be attributable to authors who take responsibility for them. That tension is now live. Our own read, flagged as analysis: how the community handles a machine-checked proof nobody can read will shape how AI results are judged for years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Did AI solve the Riemann Hypothesis?

Not confirmed. Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge claims that he and Claude found a proof, released on November 20, 2026 as Lean code. Reports say the code compiles, but a check that it proves the hypothesis as stated was pending, there is no human-readable proof, and it hasn't been peer reviewed. The Clay Mathematics Institute still listed the problem as unsolved on November 20.

Who is Levent Alpöge?

A mathematician at Anthropic, described by one source as a junior fellow at the Harvard Society of Fellows. He previously worked on Anthropic's August result about zeros of the zeta function. He posted the claim on his personal website.

Is this an official Anthropic announcement?

The coverage we read reports no official Anthropic statement. Alpöge's own note says the decision to go public was his, and reports say he messaged CEO Dario Amodei beforehand. It is presented as a researcher's claim.

What is the Riemann Hypothesis in simple terms?

It says that all the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function lie on a single line, where the real part equals one-half. Those zeros are linked to how prime numbers are distributed. Riemann proposed it in 1859, and it has stayed unproven for about 167 years.

What is Lean, and does a compiled Lean proof settle it?

Lean is a proof assistant that checks every logical step by computer. A compiling proof means the steps are valid for the statement written in the code. Experts still need to confirm that statement matches the real Riemann Hypothesis and that no definitions were quietly changed. Those checks were still ongoing in the reports we read.

Has anyone verified Alpöge's proof?

Partly. Quanta reports that Andrew Sutherland (MIT) compiled the Lean code first and Simon DeDeo (Carnegie Mellon) later did so independently. A final comparator check and redefinition checks were still pending, and no human has verified the underlying mathematics in the usual way.

What do mathematicians say about it?

Views are mixed. Per The Next Web, Sutherland said the chance of an error is "approximately zero," and James Maynard said he was "98 or 99 percent" certain. Per Quanta, Maksym Radziwill said the Lean proof "short-circuits understanding," while Filippo Nuccio called the argument "gorgeous." Brian Conrad criticized releasing a proof the author doesn't fully understand.

Will Anthropic or Alpöge win the $1 million prize?

Not soon. Per Kingy.ai's reading of Clay's rules, a claim needs a qualifying publication, a two-year wait and general acceptance before a prize is considered. We didn't read the rules directly. No prize has been awarded.

How is this different from Anthropic's August Riemann result?

In August, Anthropic said an unreleased Claude model raised the proven share of zeros on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2% and explicitly said it did not solve the hypothesis. The November claim is a purported full proof, with far less verification so far.

When will we know if the proof is right?

Quick Lean checks may finish within days, but community understanding could take months or years. The comparator and redefinition checks are the first things to watch. Full acceptance would require experts to follow the argument and, normally, formal publication.

Our Verdict

// OUR TAKE — THE RIEMANN HYPOTHESIS CLAIM

This is a serious claim that deserves attention and not yet belief. A working Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude proved it, the Lean code has reportedly compiled on independent machines, and some experts sound close to convinced. But the key statement check was pending, nobody can read the proof, and no one has refereed it. "AI solved the Riemann Hypothesis" is premature. "An AI-assisted proof is being checked" is accurate.
